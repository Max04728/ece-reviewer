# 07 — PD 1586, EIS and ECC

> [!abstract] Scope
> Know which projects and areas fall under the Environmental Impact Statement System, what each document (EIS, IEE, ECC, CNC) is and who issues it, and the validity and penalty rules an examiner asks about.

## Core Concept

> [!tip] Intuition
> PD 1586 is a gate, not a tax: before a project with significant environmental impact can be built, someone must predict its impact on paper, a review committee must accept that prediction, and the certificate that follows becomes a set of enforceable commitments for the life of the project.

**The statute.** Presidential Decree No. 1586, signed **11 June 1978**, established the **Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) System** and is the legal basis of environmental impact assessment in the Philippines. It built on PD 1151 (Philippine Environmental Policy, 1977) and PD 1152 (Philippine Environment Code, 1977), which first required environmental impact statements of government agencies and instrumentalities. Section 2 of PD 1586 anchors the system on that requirement; **Section 4** is the operative prohibition: no person, partnership or corporation may undertake or operate a declared environmentally critical project or area **without first securing an Environmental Compliance Certificate (ECC)** issued by the President or a duly authorised representative. **Section 5** provides that projects and areas **not** declared environmentally critical are not required to submit an EIS, though the then-National Environmental Protection Council could require additional safeguards. **Section 9** carries the penalty: suspension or cancellation of the certificate and/or a fine **not exceeding P50,000 for every violation**, at the discretion of the Council.

**Environmentally critical projects and areas.** The declaration was made by **Proclamation No. 2146 (14 December 1981)**, which lists the Environmentally Critical Projects (ECPs) in three groups — (I) heavy industries: non-ferrous metal industries, iron and steel mills, petroleum and petrochemical industries including oil and gas, and smelting plants; (II) resource extractive industries: major mining and quarrying, forestry projects (logging, major wood processing, introduction of exotic fauna, forest occupancy, mangrove extraction, grazing) and fishery projects including dikes and fishpond development; (III) infrastructure projects: major dams, major power plants (fossil, nuclear, hydroelectric or geothermal), major reclamation projects, and major roads and bridges. It also lists twelve categories of **Environmentally Critical Areas (ECAs)**: national parks, watershed reserves, wildlife preserves and sanctuaries; aesthetic potential tourist spots; habitat of endangered or threatened indigenous species; areas of unique historic, archaeological or scientific interest; areas traditionally occupied by cultural communities; areas frequently hit by natural calamities; areas with critical slopes; prime agricultural lands; recharged areas of aquifers; water bodies tapped for domestic purposes, in protected areas, or supporting wildlife and fishery; mangrove areas with primary growth, adjoining major river mouths, adjacent to fishing grounds, acting as buffers, or supporting livelihoods; and coral reefs with 50% or more live coral cover, spawning or nursery grounds, or acting as natural breakwaters.

**The document set and the permits.** The EIA system produces four things the examiner distinguishes. An **Environmental Impact Statement (EIS)** is the full, detailed study of a project's environmental impact, prepared by the proponent, with an environmental management and monitoring plan; it is required for an ECP or a project in an ECA. An **Initial Environmental Examination (IEE)** is the shorter screening-level study used for projects that are not ECPs or are outside ECAs but that may still cause significant impact. A **Project Description (PD)** is the minimal submission for projects with no significant impact. The **Environmental Compliance Certificate (ECC)** is the approval issued after review — it certifies that the project may proceed subject to stated conditions, and those conditions include the environmental management plan, monitoring and submission of compliance reports, a pollution control officer, and sometimes a social development or resettlement commitment. A **Certificate of Non-Coverage (CNC)** is issued when a project is **not** covered by the EIS System (for example, outside an ECA and not an ECP), and it confirms that no ECC is required. The **DENR-EMB** is the implementing agency; the **EIA Review Committee (EIARC)**, a multi-disciplinary peer body, evaluates EIS submissions, and the ECC is approved by the DENR Secretary for ECPs and by the EMB regional offices for other covered projects, with the **Environmental Management Bureau** acting as secretariat. DAO 2003-30, as amended by DAO 2017-15, is the current procedural manual for the system.

**Validity, conditions and consequences.** An ECC is valid for the life of the project unless suspended or revoked, but its conditions bind the proponent: any change in the project's scope, design or process that alters its impact requires the ECC to be amended, and a project that has not started within the period stated in the ECC must secure an extension. Non-compliance with ECC conditions, or undertaking a covered project without an ECC, exposes the proponent to suspension or cancellation of the certificate and/or a fine of up to P50,000 per violation under Section 9, and PD 1586 violations are also expressly listed as prohibited acts under RA 9275 §27(h) for water-related projects. Two common confusions to hold apart: the **ECC is not a discharge permit** (that is RA 9275, DENR-EMB), and it is not a permit to operate a solid waste facility in itself (RA 9003 §38 still requires the ECC plus the other clearances) — but it is a prerequisite for both.

## Traps & Exam Notes

- Quoting the EIS System as established by PD 1151 or PD 1152. PD 1586 (11 June 1978) established the system; PD 1151 is the environmental policy and PD 1152 the environment code, and both are earlier (1977).
- Saying the ECC is issued by the President. Section 4 allows the President **or a duly authorised representative**; in practice the DENR Secretary issues ECCs for ECPs and the EMB regional offices for other covered projects.
- Mixing up the EIS and the IEE. The EIS is the full study for an ECP or an ECA project; the IEE is the screening-level document used elsewhere. Submitting an IEE for an ECP is a procedural defect, not a shortcut.
- Treating a CNC as a weaker ECC. A CNC says the project is **not covered** and no ECC is required; a project holds one or the other. Applying for an ECC 'just in case' when a CNC is proper is a different error from operating without one.
- Quoting the PD 1586 fine as P50,000 **per day**. Section 9 says up to P50,000 **for every violation** — the per-day structure belongs to RA 8749 (up to P100,000/day) and RA 9275 (P10,000 to P200,000/day).
- Calling Proclamation 2146 a DENR administrative order. It is a presidential proclamation of 14 December 1981, and it is the instrument that actually lists the ECPs and ECAs.
- Listing only projects and forgetting the areas. ECPs and ECAs are two separate limbs of coverage; a modest project inside a critical area (say a watershed reserve or a coral reef with 50%+ live cover) still needs an ECC.
- Assuming the ECC never expires and never needs amending. It runs for the life of the project, but scope or process changes require an amendment and a delayed start requires the extension described in the certificate.
