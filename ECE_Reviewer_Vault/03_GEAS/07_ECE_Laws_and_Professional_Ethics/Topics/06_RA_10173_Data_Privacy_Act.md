# 06 — RA 10173 Data Privacy Act

> [!abstract] Scope
> What RA 10173 protects, who enforces it, which categories of data trigger the heavier penalties, and the exact fine and imprisonment ranges for each offence.

## Core Concept

> [!tip] Intuition
> Two axes decide everything under the Data Privacy Act: what kind of data it is (personal or sensitive personal) and what the controller did with it (processed, exposed by negligence, disposed of, misused, breached or concealed).

**Who enforces it.** The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173, approved 15 August 2012) created the **National Privacy Commission**, an independent body attached to the Department of Information and Communications Technology and headed by a Privacy Commissioner who also chairs it, assisted by two (2) Deputy Privacy Commissioners — one for Data Processing Systems and one for Policies and Planning — all appointed by the President for a term of three (3) years, reappointable for another three (3) years (RA 10173 §9). The Commission's functions (§7) include ensuring compliance by personal information controllers, receiving complaints and instituting investigations, acting as a **collegial body** in resolving complaints and investigations except where an amicable settlement is reached, issuing cease and desist orders and imposing a temporary or permanent ban on processing where the processing will be detrimental to national security and public interest, adjudicating and awarding indemnity, compelling any entity or agency to abide by its orders, and **recommending to the Department of Justice the prosecution and imposition of the penalties** under §§25 to 29.

**The two data classes.** *Personal information* (§3(g)) is any information, recorded in material form or not, from which the identity of an individual is apparent or can be reasonably and directly ascertained by the entity holding it, or which when put together with other information would directly and certainly identify an individual. *Sensitive personal information* (§3(l)) is narrower and enumerated: (1) race, ethnic origin, marital status, age, colour, and religious, philosophical or political affiliations; (2) health, education, genetic or sexual life, or any proceeding for an offence committed or alleged, its disposal, or the sentence of any court in such proceedings; (3) information issued by government agencies peculiar to an individual, including social security numbers, previous or current health records, licences or their denials, suspension or revocation, and tax returns; and (4) information specifically established by an executive order or an act of Congress to be kept classified. *Privileged information* (§3(k)) is data which under the Rules of Court and other pertinent laws constitutes privileged communication. The class matters because it drives both the lawful-basis test and the penalty.

**Principles, lawful bases and the sensitive-data exception.** Processing is allowed subject to adherence to the principles of **transparency, legitimate purpose and proportionality**, and personal information must be collected for specified and legitimate purposes declared before or as soon as practicable after collection and later processed only compatibly with them; processed fairly and lawfully; accurate, relevant and kept up to date; adequate and not excessive; retained only as long as necessary; and kept in a form permitting identification of data subjects for no longer than necessary (§11). Under §12, processing of personal information is permitted only if at least one of six conditions exists: consent of the data subject; necessity for the fulfilment of a contract with the data subject or steps prior to a contract; compliance with a legal obligation of the controller; protection of vitally important interests of the data subject including life and health; response to a national emergency, compliance with public order and safety requirements, or fulfilment of public authority functions; or the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party to whom the data is disclosed, **except where those interests are overridden by the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject**. Sensitive personal information and privileged information are **prohibited from processing** unless an exception in §13 applies — the principal ones being consent specific to the purpose given prior to processing, processing provided for by existing laws and regulations that guarantee protection, necessity to protect the life and health of the data subject or another person where the data subject cannot express consent, and other narrowly drawn cases.

**Data subject rights.** Chapter IV gives the data subject the right to be informed whether personal information pertaining to him is being or has been processed, and to be furnished the description, purposes, scope, method, recipients, sources and the identity of the controller; the right to object to the processing of his personal information; the right to reasonable access, upon demand, to the contents of his personal information, the sources from which it was obtained, the names and addresses of the recipients, the manner in which it was processed, and the reasons for disclosure; the right to dispute the inaccuracy or error and to have it immediately corrected, with the controller obliged to inform third parties who previously received it; the right to suspend, withdraw or order the **blocking, removal or destruction** of his personal information from the controller's filing system upon discovery and substantial proof that it is incomplete, outdated, false, unlawfully obtained, used for unauthorised purposes or no longer necessary; and the right to be **indemnified for damages** sustained due to inaccurate, incomplete, outdated, false, unlawfully obtained or unauthorised use of his personal information (§16). The rights are transmissible to the lawful heirs and assigns of the data subject (§17), and the data subject has the separate right to **data portability** — to obtain a copy of data undergoing processing in an electronic or structured format that is commonly used and allows further use, where the personal information is processed by electronic means (§18).

**Obligations of controllers and processors.** The personal information controller must implement reasonable and appropriate **organisational, physical and technical** measures for protection against accidental or unlawful destruction, alteration and disclosure and against other unlawful processing, taking into account the nature of the data, the risks of the processing, the size and complexity of the organisation, current data privacy best practices and the cost of implementation; the measures must include safeguards for the computer network, a security policy, a process for identifying and addressing foreseeable vulnerabilities, and regular monitoring for security breaches (§20). It must ensure that third parties processing on its behalf implement the same measures, and persons involved in processing hold the information under strict confidentiality, an obligation that continues after leaving the service or terminating employment. Under the principle of accountability (§21), the controller is responsible for personal information under its control or custody, including information transferred to a third party domestically or internationally, must use contractual or other reasonable means to provide a comparable level of protection, and **must designate an individual or individuals accountable for the organisation's compliance** — the Data Protection Officer — whose identity is made known to any data subject on request. Government heads of agencies are responsible for securing sensitive personal information (§22); access by government personnel requires a security clearance, off-site access needs written approval by the head of the agency with a limit of not more than **one thousand (1,000) records** at a time and encryption of the technology used (§23); and a government contract that may involve accessing or requiring sensitive personal information of **one thousand (1,000) or more individuals** requires the contractor and its employees to **register their personal information processing system with the Commission** (§24). A controller may outsource processing to a personal information processor (§3(i)).

**Breach notification and the 72-hour rule.** The statute itself (§20(f)) requires the controller to notify the Commission and affected data subjects **promptly** when sensitive personal information, or other information that may be used to enable identity fraud, is reasonably believed to have been acquired by an unauthorised person and the controller or the Commission believes the unauthorised acquisition is likely to give rise to a **real risk of serious harm** to any affected data subject; the notification must at minimum describe the nature of the breach, the sensitive personal information possibly involved, and the measures taken. Notification may be delayed only to the extent necessary to determine the scope of the breach, to prevent further disclosures, or to restore reasonable integrity to the information and communications system; the Commission may exempt a controller from notification where it would not be in the public interest or the interests of the data subjects, and may authorise postponement where notification would hinder a criminal investigation. The familiar **72-hour** figure is not in the statute — it is the deadline in the National Privacy Commission's own breach-notification rules (NPC Circular 16-03), made under the Commission's rule-making power, and concealment of a breach the controller knows about is itself a criminal offence under §30.

**The penalty table.** Chapter VIII is the densest examinable list in the law, and each row keys off the data class. Unauthorised processing (§25): personal information — imprisonment of one (1) year to three (3) years and a fine of not less than ₱500,000 but not more than ₱2,000,000; sensitive personal information — three (3) years to six (6) years and ₱500,000 to ₱4,000,000. Accessing personal or sensitive personal information **due to negligence** (§26): the identical ranges as §25. Improper disposal (§27): personal information — six (6) months to two (2) years and ₱100,000 to ₱500,000; sensitive personal information — one (1) year to three (3) years and ₱100,000 to ₱1,000,000. Processing for unauthorised purposes (§28): personal information — one (1) year and six (6) months to five (5) years and ₱500,000 to ₱1,000,000; sensitive personal information — two (2) years to seven (7) years and ₱500,000 to ₱2,000,000. Unauthorised access or intentional breach (§29): one (1) year to three (3) years and ₱500,000 to ₱2,000,000. Concealment of a security breach involving sensitive personal information (§30): one (1) year and six (6) months to five (5) years and ₱500,000 to ₱1,000,000. Malicious disclosure (§31) and unauthorised disclosure of personal information (§32(a)): one (1) year and six (6) months to five (5) years and ₱500,000 to ₱1,000,000 for malicious disclosure, and one (1) year to three (3) years and ₱500,000 to ₱1,000,000 for unauthorised disclosure; unauthorised disclosure of **sensitive** personal information (§32(b)): three (3) years to five (5) years and ₱500,000 to ₱2,000,000. Combination or series of acts under §§25 to 32 (§33): three (3) years to six (6) years and ₱1,000,000 to ₱5,000,000.

**Extent of liability and aggravating rules.** If the offender is a corporation, partnership or other juridical person, the penalty is imposed on the responsible officers who participated in or by gross negligence allowed the commission of the crime, and the court may suspend or revoke the juridical person's rights under the Act; an alien offender is deported without further proceedings after serving the penalty; a public official or employee found guilty under §27 or §28 suffers perpetual or temporary absolute disqualification from office in addition to the prescribed penalties (§34). Under §35, the **maximum penalty** in the applicable scale must be imposed when the personal information of at least **one hundred (100) persons** is harmed, affected or involved. Under §36, when the offender is a public officer as defined in the Administrative Code acting in the exercise of his duties, an accessory penalty of disqualification from public office applies for a term **double** the term of the criminal penalty imposed. Restitution is governed by the New Civil Code (§37), and any doubt in interpretation is resolved liberally in favour of the data subject (§38).

## Traps & Exam Notes

- Answering “PRC” when asked who investigates a data-privacy complaint: the National Privacy Commission (RA 10173 §7) is the privacy regulator and receives complaints, institutes investigations and adjudicates; the PRC only handles a professional's licence.
- Attributing the 72-hour breach notification deadline to the text of RA 10173. Section 20(f) says **promptly** and sets the trigger (unauthorised acquisition plus a real risk of serious harm); the 72-hour deadline is imposed by the NPC's breach-notification circular issued under its rule-making power.
- Swapping the sensitive-data fine ceiling. Unauthorised processing of sensitive personal information is ₱500,000 to **₱4,000,000**, not ₱2,000,000; ₱2,000,000 is the personal-information ceiling under §25(a).
- Treating access due to negligence as a lesser offence. RA 10173 §26 carries the **same** imprisonment and fine ranges as deliberate unauthorised processing under §25 — negligence is not a mitigation in the penalty table.
- Saying the maximum penalty applies when 1,000 persons are affected. The large-scale threshold in §35 is at least **one hundred (100)** persons; the 1,000 figure belongs to the government off-site access limit (§23) and the contractor registration trigger (§24).
- Answering that a public officer who offends is merely fined. Under §34 he also suffers perpetual or temporary absolute disqualification from office for offences under §§27 and 28, and under §36 a public officer acting in the exercise of his duties suffers disqualification for **double** the term of the criminal penalty.
- Processing sensitive personal information on the strength of the general legitimate-interests basis. The six lawful bases in §12 govern personal information; sensitive personal information and privileged information are prohibited from processing unless a §13 exception applies, and consent there must be specific to the purpose and given before processing.
- Confusing the NPC's enforcement route with prosecution: the Commission recommends prosecution and the imposition of penalties to the **Department of Justice** under §7(i); it does not itself file the criminal information.
