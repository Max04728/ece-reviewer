### 1. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Mixing up the cut-off direction for percentiles.** A top-10% cut-off uses $z=+1.282$ (left area 0.90), a bottom-5% limit uses $z=-1.645$. Reversing the sign puts the limit on the wrong side of the mean entirely.

### 2. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **The wrong critical value for the confidence level.** 95% two-sided is 1.960, not 1.645 (90%) and not 2.576 (99%). A mismatch changes the interval width by up to 57%.

<sub>from MATH-09-11</sub>

### 3. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Confusing the mean and variance of the Poisson.** They are both $\lambda$. If a problem gives a variance and asks for the mean, no computation is needed — and if observed data show variance much greater than the mean, the Poisson model does not fit.

<sub>from MATH-09-06</sub>

### 4. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Applying the normal to small counts or heavily skewed data.** With $np<5$ the binomial's normal approximation is poor — use the exact binomial or the Poisson, and remember the continuity correction when a discrete variable is approximated by a continuous one.

<sub>from MATH-09-08</sub>

### 5. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Forgetting that zero cannot lead a number.** In PIN and number-formation problems with digits, the first position has one fewer choice than the rest — and zero returns to the pool afterwards.

<sub>from MATH-09-03</sub>

### 6. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Using $\hat p$ in the standard error of a proportion test.** Tests assume $H_0$, so the standard error uses $p_0$. Confidence intervals use $\hat p$. Mixing the two conventions is the most common numerical error in proportion testing.

<sub>from MATH-09-12</sub>

### 7. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Dropping the $q^{n-k}$ factor in the binomial pmf.** $\binom{n}{k}p^{k}$ alone is only the probability of one specific ordered sequence; the binomial coefficient counts the orderings and the $q$ power accounts for the failures.

<sub>from MATH-09-05</sub>

### 8. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Dividing by $n$ instead of $\sqrt n$ in the standard error.** The single most common numerical error in the topic. The standard error of a mean is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$; quadrupling $n$ halves it.

<sub>from MATH-09-09</sub>

### 9. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **No replication and then computing error.** With one observation per cell in a two-way design the interaction is not estimable; the residual must be used as error and the no-interaction assumption stated. In a one-way design with one observation per group there is no error term at all and no test is possible.

<sub>from MATH-09-14</sub>

### 10. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Treating a circular arrangement as linear.** $(n-1)!$, not $n!$. If clockwise and anticlockwise are distinguished, do not divide by 2; if the question says 'necklace' or 'bracelet', do.

<sub>from MATH-09-03</sub>

### 11. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Multiplying without adjusting for sampling without replacement.** After drawing one ball from 9 the next draw is from 8; using $5/9$ twice treats the draws as independent when they are not.

<sub>from MATH-09-04</sub>

### 12. What goes wrong here?

> [!success]- Trap
> **Computing $P(\text{at least one})$ by summing cases.** The complement $1-P(\text{none})$ is shorter and avoids the double-counting error that case-splitting invites.
